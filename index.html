Follow the packet

Everything you send is cut into small pieces called packets. Pick something you do every day and ride one.

A message

  1. Your phoneyou type & tap send
  2. Your Wi-Fifirst hop out
  3. The interneta relay of networks
  4. Chat serverssort & hold it
  5. Friend’s phoneit lands
  1. You tap “Send” Your message is cut into small numbered pieces — packets — like posting a long letter as several postcards, each marked “1 of 5”, “2 of 5”, and so on. Packetisation: the app segments the payload and stamps each segment with source/destination addresses and a sequence number (IP + transport headers).
  2. First hop: your router Every packet leaves through the same front door — your router — which passes it to your internet provider. A slow or badly placed router delays every single packet. The router performs NAT and forwards packets to the ISP gateway using its routing table; Wi-Fi is only this first local hop.
  3. Across the internet Packets race across many networks, often by different routes. It doesn’t matter if postcard 3 arrives before postcard 2 — the numbers fix the order at the end. Routers forward by destination IP along independent paths; TCP reorders segments and retransmits any that are lost.
  4. The chat service sorts it The app’s servers receive the packets, rebuild the message, store it safely, and get ready to deliver it — even if your friend is offline right now. The service reassembles the stream, persists the message, and queues a push notification for the recipient’s device.
  5. It lands on their phone Their phone downloads the packets, rebuilds the message in order, and shows it — usually in well under a second, across several networks you never see. Delivery is confirmed end-to-end; read receipts and “typing…” indicators travel back the same way as tiny packets of their own.

A website

  1. Your browseryou type an address
  2. Name lookupfinds the server
  3. The internetpackets travel
  4. Web serverbuilds the page
  5. Your screenpage appears
  1. You type an address A name like a shop sign is easier to remember than a street address. Your browser first has to translate that name into the server’s actual numeric address. A DNS resolver maps the domain to an IP address, usually from cache; this lookup happens before any page data moves.
  2. Your browser asks politely It opens a secure, encrypted conversation with the server and asks: “Please send me this page.” Nobody in between can read what passes. A TCP connection plus a TLS handshake establishes encryption, then an HTTP request is sent — modern sites use HTTP/2 or HTTP/3.
  3. The page travels in packets The page isn’t one file — it’s text, photos, and code arriving as many packets, then rebuilt in your browser in the right order. The response is segmented, transferred, and reassembled; assets are often served in parallel from a CDN near you.
  4. The server does its work Behind the scenes the server may check a database, personalise the page, or confirm you’re logged in — all before the packets even start back. Server-side code (for example PHP) queries a database, renders HTML, and returns it with status codes and headers.
  5. Your browser builds the page It lays out text, loads images, and runs the page’s code. A “slow website” is often slow right here — too much to download or build on a modest phone. The browser parses HTML into the DOM, applies CSS, executes JavaScript, and paints; heavy assets block first paint.

A payment

  1. Checkout pageyou confirm
  2. Encryptiondetails sealed
  3. Payment gatewaychecks & routes
  4. Your bankapproves or not
  5. Receiptboth sides record it
  1. You press “Pay” Only the details needed for this purchase are sent — and never as plain readable text travelling openly across the internet. The form submits over HTTPS; reputable checkouts tokenise card data so the merchant never stores the full number.
  2. Everything gets sealed Your device scrambles the details into a code that only the payment service can unscramble. Anyone intercepting it sees nonsense. TLS encrypts in transit; the gateway exchanges tokens with the card network rather than raw card numbers.
  3. A middleman checks the route A payment gateway verifies the details are well-formed, checks for obvious fraud signals, and routes the request to the right bank. The gateway calls the acquiring bank → card network → issuing bank, carrying amount, currency, and risk signals.
  4. Your bank decides The bank checks it’s really you (sometimes asking for a one-time code), that funds exist, and that nothing looks unusual — then approves or declines in seconds. The issuer runs fraud scoring and may trigger 3-D Secure (an OTP/app approval) before returning an authorisation code.
  5. Both sides keep the receipt You get a confirmation and the shop gets paid later in a batch. That reference number is your proof if anything needs tracing. Authorisation is followed by clearing and settlement; the transaction reference links every record across systems.

A file

  1. Your laptopyou attach it
  2. Uploadcut into pieces
  3. The internetpieces travel
  4. Cloud storagecopies kept
  5. Teacher’s screenfile opens
  1. You attach the file The file is read from your device and prepared for sending, like putting a thick report into several envelopes that each fit through the mail slot. The browser reads the file as a byte stream and splits it into chunks (often a few MB each) so a failed chunk can be retried without restarting the upload.
  2. It uploads in pieces Each piece is numbered and sent with a label saying where it is going. If your connection hiccups, only the missing piece needs to be sent again. Chunks are sent as HTTPS requests; each is checksummed so the server can detect corruption and ask for a resend.
  3. Across the internet The pieces take whatever routes are free at that moment. Your upload speed, often much lower than your download speed, sets how fast this part goes. Packets are routed independently; throughput is capped by the slowest link, which at home is usually the upstream side of the connection.
  4. Storage keeps copies The service reassembles the file and stores it, usually in more than one place, so one broken disk does not mean a lost file. The service verifies the whole-file hash, writes to object storage, and replicates it across disks or data centres.
  5. Your teacher opens it When your teacher clicks the link, the same journey runs in reverse: the pieces come down, are put back in order, and the file opens exactly as you saved it. A download is served from storage, often via a nearby cache; the client checks the size and hash before opening it.

What you will learn here

  • How everyday things travel

    A message, a web page, a payment and a file all move as small packets. You will see each step, with no jargon.

  • What can go wrong

    Slow Wi-Fi, lost files, copycat checkout pages. You will learn to spot the cause in minutes.

  • One thing to try today

    Every journey ends with a small action, such as turning on updates or backing up a folder.

  1. Turn on automatic updates

    Updates are mostly security fixes wearing a boring costume. The week a flaw becomes public is the week attackers start using it — updated devices are already patched.

    Tonight: open your phone’s settings, find Software Update, and switch on “download and install automatically”.

  2. Use a password manager

    Nobody can memorise 60 strong, different passwords — so everybody reuses a few weak ones. A manager remembers the strong ones for you; you remember one master password.

    Pick a reputable manager, then change your email password first — it’s the master key to everything else.

  3. Switch on two-factor authentication (2FA)

    A password can be guessed or leaked. A second factor — a code or approval on your phone — means a stolen password alone still isn’t enough to get in.

    Enable 2FA on your email and social accounts today. Prefer an authenticator app over SMS codes when offered.

  4. Back up the files you’d cry over

    Phones get lost, laptops die, files get deleted. A backup turns a disaster into an inconvenience — but only if it exists before you need it.

    Copy your most important folder to a cloud drive AND a USB stick today. Then open the cloud copy to prove it works.

  5. Restart your router properly, monthly

    Routers are small computers that run for months without a break; memory fills and small faults pile up. A monthly restart clears them and installs a fresh connection.

    Unplug the router for 10 full seconds, plug it back in, and give it two minutes. Note the date — repeat monthly.

  6. Slow down for urgent messages

    Every scam shares one ingredient: hurry. “Act now”, “last chance”, “account closing” — urgency is manufactured to stop you checking.

    Next urgent message you get: do nothing for 10 minutes, then verify through the official app or website — never the message’s own link.
